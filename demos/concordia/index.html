Initiative to Tackle Isolation Amongst Older Men Debuts in NDG

Men's Shed Canada set up a new chapter in NDG on January 23, 2026, hoping to bring men feeling secluded together.

The Montreal West United Church was the location at which NDG's inaugural Men's Shed meeting took place last week, January 23, 2026. The Men's Shed is an international organization with thousands of locations worldwide. Its goal is to resolve an issue that has been plaguing men, especially those of past generations.

The initiative to set one up in NDG was spearheaded by Kaden Nelson, who works for the Eva Marsden Centre. Previously known as the N.D.G. Senior Citizens' Council, the Eva Marsden Centre advocates for the respect and empowerment of older people.

"I think it's quite broadly understood that in the society we have and the structures we have, men tend to be isolated. They tend to repress their emotions and not communicate."

— Doran Smith, father & retiree, NDG Men's Shed inaugural meeting

Although the project is not focused on younger men, Doran and Roman Smith — a father and son who attended the inaugural meeting — hope that intergenerational learning can be a large part of the organization moving forward. In many Men's Sheds there are people of all ages, some as young as 11.

"I think it's important to have more intergenerational spaces where people can learn from other generations. And it goes both ways too — it's not just passing down stuff; it's also learning from people who are younger with different perspectives."

— Doran Smith

His son Roman added a perspective that speaks to the quiet crisis facing younger men too: "I feel like the guidance we have right next to us — like our parents, friends and teachers — goes over our heads most of the time. Taking the time with my dad to really talk about things like relationships or mental health really puts things into perspective."

In Their Own Words

Voices from the Men's Shed

The following are excerpts from interviews conducted at NDG's inaugural Men's Shed meeting on January 23, 2026.

0:00 —

Interview — Kaden Nelson, Program Co-ordinator, Eva Marsden Centre

On what contributes most to older men's isolation

"I think what mostly contributes to that is their mental health. It's key. If they're living with their wife and the wife passes away, they tend to isolate themselves. Their physical abilities, socioeconomic status — people tend to stay away from programs due to all of that. So it's basically the socioeconomic status of the individual and other comorbidities as well."

On how the Men's Shed tackles the problem

"It aims to tackle that by bringing men together in a space where they can share ideas, communicate with each other, and build a support network so they can help each other out in times of need."

On his vision for the future

"My vision for this Men's Shed is to expand, get as many participants as possible, so that in case I'm not in the organization, there's someone ahead who can lead the way and just keep the group going — because it would be beneficial for them where they can come out, get coffee, or do something they enjoy. At the end of the day, it's for them to be part of something, to be supported, welcomed and loved."

On breaking the emotional barrier

"I think if we have more people emotionally expressing themselves, it will be much easier to connect with that individual. If you tend to withhold yourself, it's kind of hard to break that barrier. If people were more open — able to share — that will be fine."

"Groups give people a place to go, and that matters more than we often realize."

— Kregg Hetherington, Anthropology & Sociology, Concordia University
0:00 —

Interview — Doran & Roman Smith, Father & Son

Doran — On the breakdown of intergenerational bonds

"Before the Industrial Revolution, the father would transmit his skills and his business to the son, and then they would take over. That's how things used to be. We don't really have that structure anymore. So I think it's important to have intergenerational spaces where people can learn from other generations — and it goes both ways."

Roman, age 17 — On navigating early adulthood

"As a young guy going into the world, you still don't really know what you want to do. The guidance right next to us — parents, friends, teachers — goes over our heads most of the time. Taking the time with my dad to really talk about things like relationships or mental health really puts things into perspective and helps me find out what I truly want."

Doran — On why communities like the Men's Shed matter

"We had a huge breakdown of community. A huge breakdown of living together, supporting each other, knowing our neighbors. I don't think there is any way forward as a society unless we go back to that. Men feel good when they contribute, when they give of what they can give, and it's appreciated. That is a very, very important thing for people to experience."

On whether government does enough for men's mental health

"The answer is no, because it's still such a big problem. If we look at root causes, things like the Men's Shed are trying to directly attack the isolation and the lack of value, the lack of identity — and provide those things. That seems to go more for the root causes, which is definitely the right way to do it."

"They talked openly about people who are lonely, depressed, or lacking confidence and that impressed me."

— Douglas Dayton, attending his first Men's Shed meeting
