In Depth

What Is the Manosphere — and Why Does It Matter?

A radicalization ecosystem is pulling young men toward increasingly extreme views. Understanding how it works is the first step to dismantling it.

When people hear the word "incel" they often think of fringe online communities, dark subreddits, and isolated acts of violence. But the phenomenon is neither fringe nor isolated. It is the endpoint of a long pipeline — and that pipeline is moving closer to the mainstream every year.

The Manosphere

The community of influencers and their followers who are convinced that society is moving in the wrong direction and hold misogynistic, often alt-right views. It encompasses everything from self-help gurus to outright hate communities, united by a shared resentment toward feminism and a belief that men are the true victims of modern society.

According to an article from the Guardian, Gen-Z men are twice as likely to believe that women should obey their husbands than baby boomers. This comes as many young men are fed content from self-proclaimed "pick-up artists" or alpha males who preach very traditional views on women's gender roles — often through recommendation algorithms that have no concept of the damage they do.

The Black Pill Pipeline

Incels — or "involuntary celibates" — are young men who believe that their inability to attain a romantic relationship is something ordained at birth, a function of genetics they cannot escape. They are often grouped as a monolith, but there are levels to it, described through a series of increasingly radical "pills."

Research Finding

A study by Robert Green and Kurt Fowler of Rutgers University and Allan Palombi of Penn State shows the rigidity of these self-formed social castes. At its core, "masculinity threat" is the perception that an external factor can threaten to expose a man's masculinity as insufficient — causing him to fail at fulfilling the societal expectations of his gender. The entry point for any prospective Incel is their lack of access to sex. This, alongside having an "epiphany" about the negative aspects of feminism, is known as being "redpilled."

Once a member has been "blackpilled" — the final, most radical stage — there is no going back to previous stages. The longer they stay within Inceldom and embrace its contextual "truths," the more extreme they become. In this framework, the hierarchy of "pills" becomes more than labels: they are the empirical fingerprints of a process that filters in the most susceptible members to that level of radicalization.

People can often perceive a kind of "status inconsistency," where they feel their current position is in conflict with where they believe they should be. If society were constructed as Incels argue it should be, these hierarchies would place them firmly at the top of the social structure. When Incels band together to acknowledge that these changes are not being enacted, this can lead to further radicalization — and eventually violence.

Case Study: Elliot Rodger and the Road to Violence

The most infamous incel crime in history was committed by Elliot Rodger, who murdered six people and injured 14 others in the Isla Vista shootings of 2014. Before carrying out the attack, he killed two roommates and a friend, then drove to a sorority house at UC Santa Barbara.

In his final video, Rodger lamented how women had ignored him throughout his college life — and announced his intention to exact "retribution." His manifesto, which ran hundreds of pages, reveals less a monster conjured from nowhere and more a boy failed at every developmental juncture: by absent male mentorship, by a culture that equated masculinity with stoicism and sexual conquest, and ultimately by a radicalization pipeline that confirmed his worst instincts rather than challenging them.

"A man who doesn't get any girls is a worthless loser." This was the worldview Rodger had fully internalized — not a personal conclusion, but a lesson absorbed from a culture that had been telling him so for years.

His story illustrates in tragic, extreme form what happens when the patriarchal values explored throughout this series — emotional repression, the equation of masculinity with dominance, the absence of healthy male community — go entirely unchecked. The same forces that isolate older men in NDG, that overwhelm young students like Dexter, find their most dangerous expression in the radicalization pipeline.

What Research Says About Solutions

Many choose to simply ignore these young men or demonize them. But researchers argue that a different approach would be more effective in bringing men out of this cycle — or preventing them from falling down the rabbit hole at all.

Research — William Bukowski, Concordia University

A study analyzing previous literature on the subject shows how people who reject certain gender roles are often happier. The rigidity of gender expectations — for both men and women — is not a source of stability. It is a source of harm.
