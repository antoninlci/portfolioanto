What Does It Take to Be a Student Now?

Students are trying to find ways to adapt to higher costs of living, tuition, and housing — often at the expense of their mental health.

Young adults seeking to find a balance between work and life are hard-pressed to do so amidst record-high prices in tuition — double what it would be had they simply risen with inflation. This issue is especially pertinent in Quebec, where out-of-province Canadian students faced 33% increases in tuition, from $9,000 to $12,000. For international students the price is even more shocking, tripling from around $12,000 in 2006 to $36,000 in 2025–26.

"Coming into the school year, I was an international student, so that was a big concern to whether I was going to stay in Montreal or live in Ottawa."

— Dexter Great, student-athlete, Concordia University

Dexter Great is a full-time student, a soccer player for the Concordia Stingers, a barber operating out of his residence common room

Atop all these issues There is also housing to contend with. In Montreal, both average rental prices and vacancy rates rose 7.2% and 2.9% respectively in 2025. These statistics reference two-bedrooms, which is what most students opt for in order to share costs. The squeeze is total and relentless.

"It is harder to be a young adult today than it was some decades ago."

— Paul Kershaw, Professor, UBC School of Population and Public Health

The Invisible Toll on Mental Health

With 65% of students feeling concerned about their finances, it can be hard on someone's mental health and their prioritization of time. Experts state that the ideal amount of time a full-time student should work is 10 to 15 hours per week. But for students like Dexter, who must take care of much of their own finances, this is simply not possible.

"I feel as though I'm really overwhelmed. There's other people who are probably even doing more than I am, and still, it's crazy to think that some days I can't even find an hour or two to just decompress or be with friends."

— Dexter Great

There are paths that students from out of province can take, including select scholarships offered by universities such as Concordia for high academic achievement, or reaching a high level in your sport. However, these are highly competitive and many won't be able to secure them even if they reach the standards set by universities.

A Hustle That Never Stops

Dexter's side hustle — a barbershop that runs out of his residence's common room — began out of necessity rather than passion. He did a six-month hairstyling course before attending Concordia and practiced on his brother. He now re-invests much of his earnings back into the business: better clippers, a mirror, a TV to keep clients entertained during cuts that can run upwards of an hour.

"The way some people get help from their parents, I don't get that. So, if I want anything myself — new cleats, even textbooks — it's all on me. So it's less of I wanted to, but more of I had to."

— Dexter Great

In His Own Words

Dexter Great on Being a Student Athlete, Barber & Survivor

The following are excerpts from an interview conducted with Dexter Great, student-athlete at Concordia University.

Interview — Dexter Great, First-Year Student, Concordia University

On the financial reality of being a student today

"It feels like it's just getting more and more expensive, and it's kind of becoming like a business — they don't really care about the students, they just want to make money. Coming into the school year, I was an international student, so that was a big concern. Even now that I'm a Canadian permanent resident, like, it's still a crazy amount."

On why he started barbering in his dorm room

"Even with my textbooks and stuff, it's all on me. So yeah, it's less of like I wanted to, but more of like I had to. I don't want to stress my parents out, and either way — if I want anything myself, I gotta figure out ways to make that money."

On work-life balance as a first-year student

"I feel as though I'm really overwhelmed. And there are other people who are probably even doing more than I am. It's crazy to think that some days I can't even find an hour or two to just decompress and be with friends. I didn't do as well as I wanted to last semester, just because of the shock of — how I had to study for high school versus coming here, having to work more and study more and play more. It shouldn't have to be like this, I feel."

On the future

"I don't think this is gonna get better. I think it's gonna get worse. I feel like the government kind of sets students up for failure in a way — it's less valuable to study because of the opportunity cost. You can make so much more from just working. And as someone who wasn't able to get student loans and stuff, they're really setting you up."
